I want to spend my personal statement to briefly explain why I started from Japanese but ended up in philosophy. In high school, I was good at math and science subjects but had little interest in the humanities. Because of family pressure, however, I ended up majoring in Japanese at SISU, a pure humanities school. Its Japanese department ranks first in China, so most of my classmates arrived already speaking Japanese well, while I started with zero background. For the first two years, I was under heavy pressure just to keep up, spending most of my time memorizing words and reciting textbooks.

Because I did not choose Japanese myself, I kept looking for something else to study. At first, I tried computer science. I helped my department set up a server, did some web crawling and data analysis, and won a few small prizes. What really interested me, however, were frontier areas like natural language processing, which require systematic training in math and algorithms. With my schedule taken up by language classes, I did not have time for that training. More importantly, I realized I did not want to spend my life doing basic technical work. I grew tired of carrying out routine tasks without asking why they mattered.

Next, I turned to filmmaking, which felt closer to the Japanese literature I was studying. It taught me a lot, but I ran into a different issue. People often said that a good film or novel leaves things ambiguous and raises questions without answering them. But I wanted answers, not just questions.

Around that time, I began reading philosophy on my own, starting with Plato, Aristotle, and Kant. In their work, I finally found a field that takes deep questions seriously while still aiming for clarity and rigor. Looking back, the biggest change in how I see myself is that I used to think I should just be a technician who implements what other people ask for. Over time, I realized that others do not always know what is worth doing, and that I need to work those questions out for myself in a clear way rather than making them more obscure. Gradually, I came to see that many things we take for granted in everyday life, like our capacity for language, our intelligence, our ordinary ontological commitments, and our values, are far from clear and still lack satisfying answers. Working on these problems seriously is worth devoting my career to; it is what brought me to Brandeis for an M.A. in philosophy, and what now leads me to pursue a Ph.D. in Philosophy at the UMich.
