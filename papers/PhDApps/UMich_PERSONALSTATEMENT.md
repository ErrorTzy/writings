I want to spend my personal statement to briefly explain why I started from Japanese but ended up in philosophy. In high school, I was good at math and science subjects but had little interest in the humanities. Because of family pressure, however, I ended up majoring in Japanese at SISU, a pure humanities school. Its Japanese department ranks #1 in China, so most of my classmates already speaks good Japanese the day they enter college, while I started with no idea about Japanese alphabet. For the first two years, I was under great pressure just to keep up.

Given that I have no interest in Japanese right from the start, I kept trying different things. First, I tried computer science. I helped my department set up a server, did some web crawling and data analysis, and won trivial prizes. But what really interested me were frontier areas like natural language processing. These areas require systematic training in math and algorithms, but my time was exhausted by language classes. More importantly, I did not want to spend my life doing basic technical work. I grew tired of carrying out dirty works without asking why they mattered.

Then, I tried filmmaking, which is closer to the Japanese literature I was studying. It taught me a lot, but people often said that a good film or novel leaves things ambiguous and raises good questions without answering them. But I wanted good answers, not just good questions.

Around that time, I began reading philosophy by myself. I read Plato, Aristotle, and Kant. In their work, I finally found an area that takes hard questions seriously while still aiming for a clear and rigorous answer. Looking back, the biggest change in how I see myself is that I used to think I should just be a technician who implements what other people want. But then I realized that others do not always know what is worth doing, and that I need to work those questions out for myself in a clear way rather than making them more obscure. Gradually, I came to see that many things we take for granted in everyday life, like our capacity for language, our intelligence, our ordinary ontological commitments, and our values, are far from clear and still lack satisfying answers. Working on these problems seriously is worth devoting my career to; it is what brought me to Brandeis for an M.A. in philosophy, and what now leads me to pursue a Ph.D. in Philosophy at the UMich.
