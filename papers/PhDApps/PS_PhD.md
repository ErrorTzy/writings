**Areas of Interest**: My primary research interests lie in the philosophy of language, philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and LLM interpretability and alignment. I also have a strong interest in metaphysics, philosophy of science, philosophical logic, and Aristotle.

More specifically, within the philosophy of language, I am interested in metasemantics, radical interpretation, and the rule-following paradox. In the philosophy of mind, I am especially interested in perceptual representation and the nature of mental content. I also have a strong interest in ontological commitment, emergence, and scientific, metaphysical, and mathematical explanation.

Meanwhile, the remarkable progress in AI in recent years has deeply intrigued me. I am really cuious about how we can interpret LLM and align them. Although I have not yet taken coursework directly addressing LLM interpretability, my philosophical interests are closely related to it, and I have some background in computer science. I believe investigating LLM can shed light on how human language and the mind work on the one hand, while contributing to our ability to understand and steer LLMs on the other.

**Previous Coursework and Experience**: During my M.A. at Brandeis, I was deeply influenced by my advisor, Eli Hirsch. I cultivated my interests in the philosophy of language and metaphysics under his influence. I took 6 courses with him over 4 semesters at Brandeis. I wrote a paper arguing, contra Cappelen and Dever's account of the inessential indexical, that it is impossible for any rational agent to reduce the indexicality of the first-person pronoun. However, someone who uses the first-person pronoun non-indexically can still appear behaviorally rational. I also took Causation and Explanation with Kate Elliott, for which I wrote a paper proposing an account of hyperintensional asymmetry in metaphysical explanation. I also took Peter Epstein's graduate seminar on intentionalist and direct realist accounts of perceptual content.

My formal undergraduate coursework was largely outside of philosophy, as my university had no philosophy department or faculty. However, I audited several philosophy-related courses at Peking University, including two semesters of Introduction to Political Philosophy and a seminar on Bodin's On Sovereignty. And I studied Ancient Greek for a year. During my undergraduate years, my philosophical interests centered on Plato and Aristotle; I wrote a paper reconstructing the debate between Thrasymachus and Socrates on justice and the craft of ruling in Republic I, explaining why Socrates' argument refutes Thrasymachus' position but does not address Glaucon and Adeimantus' challenge.

I also gained experience in computer science as an undergraduate. I participated in the Asia and Pacific Mathematical Contest in Modeling and won second prize for building a neural network to predict U.S. presidential election outcomes based on economic data.
